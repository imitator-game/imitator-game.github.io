The problem

Watching is easy. Understanding is the hard part.

Someone stirs the soup

A person watches once, then does it their own way.

They do not memorise the arc of the spoon. They work out what the motion was for — the soup needs stirring — and then do that, with whatever is on the counter.

Move the pot, swap the spoon for chopsticks, hand them a wok instead of a bowl: the behaviour survives all of it, because the goal survived.

The robot copies the arc

Policies learn the motion, not the reason for it.

Most methods for learning from human video extract hand keypoints or optical flow, retarget the motion onto the arm, and replay it into a scene that looks like the one in the clip.

That works, and it is genuinely useful for bootstrapping behaviour. It also means the demonstration and the execution scene have to stay close. Nudge the pot and the plan is already wrong — even though the task never changed.

So we widen the gap

Four levels, each one further from the demonstration.

L0 keeps the scene identical. L1 rearranges it. L2 replaces the objects with different instances of the same kind. L3 replaces them with objects that work differently — a teapot becomes a tap, and the cup still has to get filled.

Nothing else changes: same task, same supervision budget, same rubric. Only the distance between what was shown and what is available.

40task–level pairs per domain, in simulation and on hardware
And the field breaks in one place

L0 to L2 barely moves. L3 is where it goes.

Across nine models, success holds around 0.40 through the first three levels and then falls to 0.29 at L3. The human imitation score falls with it. The strongest model through L2 — XSkill, at 0.56 — lands level with everything else once the affordance changes.

Rearranging a scene is survivable. Substituting an object of the same kind is survivable. Achieving the same end through a different affordance is not.

−0.11drop in success from the L0–L2 mean to L3
So we built somewhere to measure it

A benchmark, a dataset, and a room full of judges.

An automated metric that tracks human verdicts at r = 0.96, 20,000+ paired human–robot episodes, and an Arena where anyone can compare two anonymised rollouts against the clip they were both supposed to imitate.

The board is open. If your policy watches a human and does the right thing for the right reason, it should show up here.

Abstract

Humans imitate at the level of intent: given a demonstration, we infer its goal and carry it out with whatever tools, objects, and layouts are at hand. Current robot policies instead learn observation-to-action mappings from visual inputs and language instructions, without explicitly inferring the demonstrated task. Learning from human video thus remains largely trajectory-level: models can replay motions in near-identical scenes, but still struggle to imitate what the demonstrator intends rather than merely what they do. We introduce The Imitator Game, a four-level benchmark (L0–L3) that progressively widens the gap between the human demonstration and the robot's own scene, isolating where trajectory replay ceases to suffice and task understanding becomes necessary. We pair it with IG-10K, the largest environment-aligned paired human–robot dataset to date and the only one instantiated across all four levels in both real and simulated settings (20,000+ paired episodes, 50+ tasks, 6 domains), and Imitator Arena, an open platform for blind A/B human evaluation. Across nine state-of-the-art models, performance is stable from L0 to L2 but collapses at L3, identifying functional substitution — achieving the same intent through a different object affordance — as the decisive barrier to intent-level imitation. Human-video-conditioned models outperform caption-conditioned ones, yet every model falls below 13% zero-shot success on unseen tasks; fine-tuning IG-10K-pretrained models with only 10 paired human–robot demonstrations yields large gains that grow with pretraining scale.

Results

Experimental findings

We evaluate 9 state-of-the-art models (15 trained variants) across simulation, real-world deployment, and human Arena judgments — along three axes. The figures below are the paper's appendix figures, drawn live: hover any point for the underlying number.

Q1

Video conditioning beats text

Video-based methods lead on seen tasks. ACT/DINOv2 reaches SR = 0.81 in simulation; XSkill wins the real-world Arena with SR = 0.63, WR = 0.89.

Q2

Scale buys adaptation, not transfer

Every model stays below 13% zero-shot success on unseen tasks. Pretraining on IG-10K unlocks few-shot gains instead — and those gains grow with corpus size.

Q3

L3 is the barrier

Performance is flat from L0 to L2 (~0.40 SR) and then falls to 0.29 at L3. Functional substitution — the same intent through a different affordance — is unsolved.

Q1 · Fig. C1

Which imitation interface is strongest?

The paradigm landscape. Every trained variant placed on the (seen-SR, P+FT-SR) plane. The two video families cluster toward the upper right; the VLA family spreads along the diagonal, strong after fine-tuning but uneven on seen tasks. Below, the Arena win rate per paradigm — where video-skill methods lead at 0.64.

Q1 · Fig. C2

Does the visual encoder matter?

Encoder ablation. Within the video-conditioned family, DINOv2 and SigLIP2 are consistently ahead of VideoMAE, in simulation and on hardware alike. The representation is a design choice that shows up in the score, not a detail.

Q2 · Fig. C3

Does a bigger corpus help?

Corpus scaling. Going from 15 to 45 pretraining tasks improves few-shot success almost everywhere. Zero-shot is a different story: the video-conditioned models creep upward while π₀.₅ stays flat at 0.04 no matter how much paired data it sees.

Q2 · Fig. C4

Does scale help at every level?

Level × corpus size. Under pretrain + fine-tune, success rises with corpus size at every one of L0–L3, in both domains. Under zero-shot it does not move — the floor is a floor at all four levels.

Q3 · Fig. C5

Where does the hierarchy become hard?

The level profile. Real-world pretrain + fine-tune success by level, with the human imitation score on the right axis. Flat through L2, then the drop. XSkill falls furthest, because it had furthest to fall.

Fig. C7

Can you trust the automated metric?

Metric validity. The automated success rate agrees with the Arena's human verdicts at r = 0.956 across model × regime, and the sub-goal rate tracks the human imitation score just as closely. Simulation and hardware agree on ordering but not on absolute numbers — which is why the leaderboard keeps them apart.
